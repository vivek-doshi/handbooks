Step 02 · Beginner · Just enough linear algebra, calculus, probability and statistics to understand why models work

Math and statistics essentials

You do not need a mathematics degree to use ML, but a handful of ideas appear everywhere. This step teaches them through intuition and short NumPy examples, so later algorithms read as familiar pieces.

1. Vectors and matrices

A sample is a vector of features; a dataset is a matrix with one row per sample and one column per feature. Models are mostly sums and products of these.

  • Dot product: multiply matching elements and add them up. It measures how aligned two vectors are and is the core of a linear model: w . x + b.
  • Matrix multiplication: applies many dot products at once. A neural network layer is a matrix multiplication plus a non-linearity.
  • Shapes: most bugs are shape mismatches. Always check (n_samples, n_features).
  • Norms: L2 is the usual length (square root of the sum of squares); L1 is the sum of absolute values. They reappear as regularisation in step 04.
  • Eigenvectors and SVD: directions of greatest variation in data; the basis of PCA (step 07).
import numpy as np

X = np.array([[1., 2.], [3., 4.], [5., 6.]])   # 3 samples, 2 features
w = np.array([0.5, -1.0])                        # one weight per feature
b = 0.1

y_hat = X @ w + b                                # shape (3,)
print(y_hat)
print(np.linalg.norm(w), np.linalg.norm(w, 1))   # L2 and L1 norms

2. Derivatives and gradients

Training a model means minimising a loss. The derivative tells you how the loss changes if you nudge one parameter; the gradient is the vector of all those derivatives, pointing uphill. To go downhill, step in the opposite direction.

# Minimise f(w) = (w - 3)^2 by gradient descent
w, lr = 0.0, 0.1
for step in range(50):
    grad = 2 * (w - 3)      # derivative of f
    w -= lr * grad
print(w)                    # close to 3
  • Learning rate is the step size: too large overshoots and diverges, too small is slow.
  • Chain rule: the derivative of a composition is the product of derivatives. Backpropagation (step 10) is the chain rule applied layer by layer.
  • Convex vs non-convex: linear and logistic regression have one global minimum; neural networks have many, but gradient descent still works well in practice.

4. Bayes theorem

Bayes theorem updates a belief after seeing evidence:

P(class | evidence) = P(evidence | class) * P(class) / P(evidence)

Prior P(class) is what you believed before; likelihood P(evidence | class) is how well the class explains the evidence; posterior P(class | evidence) is the updated belief.

Worked example: a rare-event test

A disease affects 1% of people. A test catches 90% of cases (true positive) and wrongly flags 5% of healthy people. Given a positive test, what is the chance of disease? 0.9 * 0.01 / (0.9 * 0.01 + 0.05 * 0.99) = about 15%. Most positives are false alarms because the condition is rare. The same effect is why accuracy misleads on imbalanced data (step 08).

5. Statistics you will use daily

  • Mean, median, standard deviation, quantiles: summarise a column. The median resists outliers; the mean does not.
  • Correlation: strength of a linear relationship between two variables from -1 to 1. Correlation is not causation, and it misses non-linear relationships.
  • Sampling and the central limit theorem: averages of many samples are approximately normal, which is why confidence intervals work.
  • Confidence interval: a range that, across repeated experiments, would contain the true value a stated share of the time. Report model metrics with one rather than a single number.
  • Hypothesis test and p-value: how surprising the data would be if there were no real effect. A small p-value is evidence of an effect, not its size or importance.
  • Maximum likelihood: choose the parameters that make the observed data most probable. Linear regression with squared error and logistic regression with log loss are both maximum-likelihood fits.
import numpy as np
from scipy import stats

a = np.random.default_rng(0).normal(100, 15, 200)
b = np.random.default_rng(1).normal(105, 15, 200)
print(a.mean(), a.std(ddof=1), np.percentile(a, [25, 50, 75]))
print(stats.ttest_ind(a, b))          # compare two groups
print(np.corrcoef(a, b)[0, 1])        # correlation

6. Entropy, cross-entropy and distance

  • Entropy measures uncertainty in a distribution. Decision trees (step 06) choose splits that reduce it.
  • Cross-entropy / log loss measures how far predicted probabilities are from the true labels. It is the standard loss for classification, including neural networks and language models.
  • KL divergence measures how one distribution differs from another; it appears in variational models and in fine-tuning objectives.
  • Distances: Euclidean (straight line), Manhattan (grid), cosine (angle, used for embeddings and text). The choice defines what "similar" means for k-nearest neighbours, clustering and vector search.

  3. Compute the cosine similarity of two vectors with np.dot(a, b) / (np.linalg.norm(a) * np.linalg.norm(b)).
