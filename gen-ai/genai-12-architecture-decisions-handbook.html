Step 12 · Advanced · Fine-tune, RAG or neither

GenAI Architecture: Decisions

This is the architecture decision handbook. Given a requirement, it tells you whether to prompt, retrieve, fine-tune or combine them, which RAG strategy suits your data, and how to justify the choice with numbers.

AdvancedDecision ladderRAG strategy by scale

The decision ladder

Climb one rung at a time, and move up only when evaluation on your golden set proves the current rung is not enough.

RungTechniqueMove up when
1Better prompt, examples, structured output, a stronger or reasoning modelQuality gaps are about missing knowledge or consistent behaviour
2Add tools and context: RAG, SQL, search, APIsRetrieval is decent but behaviour, format or cost is wrong
3Improve retrieval: parsing, hybrid, rerank, query rewriting, parent-child, graph (step 05)Retrieval metrics are good and answers are still off in style, or the model is too big or slow
4Fine-tune the generator with SFT and preference tuning (steps 07 and 08)You need to specialise further or shrink the model
5Fine-tune embedder and reranker on your query and passage pairsDomain vocabulary defeats general retrieval models
6Distil a large teacher into a small studentVolume is high and a cheaper model must match the teacher on a narrow task
7Continued pretraining or training from scratchNew language or domain at huge scale; rare, expensive, usually unjustified

The one-line ruleRAG for knowledge, fine-tuning for behaviour, prompts for everything first. Most strong systems combine them: a small fine-tuned model that follows your format, fed by hybrid retrieval over fresh data.

When to fine-tune: a checklist

Fine-tune when at least three of these are true, and the prompted baseline has been measured:

  • The behaviour you need is stable and well defined (a format, a label set, a style).
  • Few-shot examples make prompts long and expensive, and you run millions of requests.
  • You have or can build 1,000 or more high-quality examples, with a held-out test set.
  • A smaller or open model must replace a large one for cost, latency, privacy or edge reasons.
  • You need reliable tool calls or strict schemas from a small model.
  • RAG already supplies the facts, and the remaining errors are about how the model responds.
  • You can operate the pipeline: data versioning, evaluation, registry, serving, retraining when the base model or data changes.

Do not fine-tune to inject frequently changing facts, to avoid building retrieval, or before you have an evaluation set.

Model selection and hosting

DecisionOptions and guidance
Frontier API versus open weightsStart with a frontier API for speed and quality. Move to open weights when data cannot leave your boundary, unit economics demand it, you need fine-tuning control, or you need offline operation.
Hosted versus self-hostedSelf-hosting needs GPUs, a serving stack, autoscaling and on-call. It tends to pay off at sustained high utilisation; otherwise managed endpoints and per-token pricing win.
Single model versus routingRoute by difficulty: small model by default, escalate on low confidence or complex intent. Typical savings are large when most traffic is easy.
Reasoning versus standard modelsUse reasoning models for planning, maths and code; standard models for retrieval Q&A, extraction and chat. Cap reasoning effort.
Context lengthPay for long context only when needed; prefer retrieval and caching.
Multimodal needsChoose models with native vision or audio when inputs are images, charts or calls; otherwise parse first.
Licences and residencyCheck commercial terms of open models, regional availability, and provider data-use policies.

Cost break-even: a worked example

Illustrative numbers only; substitute current prices and your measured token counts.

Workload: 20M requests / month
Option A: frontier model, 3,000-token prompt (instructions + few-shot examples), 300 output tokens
Option B: fine-tuned 8B model, 400-token prompt (behaviour is baked in), 300 output tokens

Cost per request = in_tokens * price_in + out_tokens * price_out
Assume A: $2.50 / 1M in, $10 / 1M out  -> 3000*2.5e-6 + 300*10e-6 = $0.0105 -> $210,000 / month
Assume B self-hosted: 8B on 4 GPUs at ~$3 / GPU-hour, always on:
        4 * 3 * 730 = $8,760 / month (+ ops, evaluation, retraining ~ $15,000 / month)
        capacity check: 20M req / 2.6M s = ~7.7 req/s average, ~25 req/s peak -> verify one GPU's tokens/s
Break-even: fine-tuning project cost (data + training + eval ~ $30,000) is recovered in weeks.
Caveat: B must match A's quality on the golden set; if it does not, the saving is not real.

Break-even drivers: request volume, prompt-length reduction, quality parity on your eval set, GPU utilisation, and engineering cost. Below roughly a few hundred thousand requests a month, API usage usually wins; above tens of millions, specialised small models usually win for narrow tasks.

Record the decision

ADR-007: Support assistant knowledge and behaviour
Context:   2.4M tickets + 300k KB articles, 40 languages, 20M requests / month, EU data residency.
Options:   (1) frontier model + RAG  (2) fine-tuned 8B + RAG  (3) fine-tuned 8B only
Evidence:  golden set of 400 questions: recall@10 = 0.93 with hybrid + rerank;
           frontier+RAG answer accuracy 0.88 at $0.0105/req; tuned 8B + RAG 0.87 at $0.0012/req.
Decision:  Option 2, frontier model as fallback for low-confidence and long-tail intents (8 percent).
Risks:     retraining when policies change; mitigated by RAG for facts, retrain quarterly for behaviour.
Review:    in 6 months or when the base model changes.

Practice

Practice
  1. Take a real requirement and walk the decision ladder, recording the metric that justifies each rung.
  2. Compute break-even for your volume with current prices.
  3. Write an ADR for your choice.
