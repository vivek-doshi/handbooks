Use Cases 6 and 7: Field Service Assistant and Catalog Enrichment

The first design is a safety-critical RAG assistant for field technicians, where the hard problems are exact identifiers, document versions and verifiable answers. The second is a high-volume extraction pipeline where retrieval is not the answer and a fine-tuned small model is. Each case shows how the approach was reached, not only what it is.

AdvancedMultimodal RAGFine-tuned SLMDecision paths

How to read these designs

Every case answers the same questions in the same order, so you can reuse the method on your own problem.

  1. What is a wrong answer worth? Cosmetic, costly or harmful errors lead to different designs.
  2. Where does the knowledge live, and how fast does it change? Frozen behaviour, private documents and live data call for different levers (step 12).
  3. What do the real inputs look like? Identifiers, tables, images and volume decide retrieval and model choices.
  4. What context do we already have? Known context (asset, tenant, category) is the cheapest way to improve quality.
  5. Which failures hurt most, and can code catch them? Programmatic verifiers beat asking a model to check itself.
  6. What does it cost at the real volume? Compare against the obvious alternative with numbers.
About the numbersAll prices, throughput and volume figures are illustrative planning numbers used to show the method. Prices below assume $0.50 per million input tokens and $2.00 per million output tokens for a mid-size hosted model, and $2.50 per GPU-hour. Replace them with measured token counts, current prices and benchmarks for your own stack.

Use case 6: Field service assistant (RAG)

ItemRequirement
BusinessIndustrial equipment maker (pumps, compressors, controllers); 12,000 field technicians in 60 countries
ContentAbout 400,000 pages of manuals, service bulletins, wiring diagrams and parts catalogues; roughly 3 million text chunks and 600,000 figures; 25 languages
Change rateWeekly manual updates; urgent safety bulletins that must be searchable within hours
QuestionsPart numbers, error codes such as E-4417, repair procedures, torque and pressure values, and photos of a nameplate or a fault display
VolumeAbout 180,000 questions a day, roughly 5 a second over working hours and 15 a second at peak
RiskA wrong torque value, a missing lockout step or a superseded procedure can injure a person or damage equipment
EnvironmentMobile devices, patchy connectivity on site, first token within about 2 seconds when online
Where the difficulty isThe corpus is small enough for one modest index. The difficulty is correctness: exact identifiers, the right document version for the right machine, complete procedures with their warnings, and answers a technician can trust with a wrench in hand.

UC6 decision path: how the approach was reached

  1. Start from the cost of an error. Because a wrong number can hurt someone, every statement must be traceable to a document the technician can open. That rules out answering from model memory, so the design must be grounded and must refuse when evidence is missing.
  2. Ask how fast knowledge changes. Manuals change weekly and safety bulletins within hours, and a fine-tuned model would be stale on release. Knowledge belongs in an index, so this is a RAG system (rung 2 of the ladder in step 12). Fine-tuning is held back for behaviour only if evaluation later shows a gap.
  3. Look at what people actually type. Many queries are exact tokens such as part numbers and error codes, which embeddings handle poorly, while others are natural-language symptoms. Retrieval therefore has to be hybrid: keyword for identifiers and dense vectors for meaning.
  4. Notice the context we already have. A work order or a nameplate photo identifies the machine: model, serial range and firmware. Filtering by that context before searching shrinks the candidate pool from millions of chunks to a few thousand. This is the largest quality and cost lever in the design.
  5. Look at the documents, not just the text. Procedures have ordered steps and warnings, torque values live in tables, and key information sits in diagrams. A plain text splitter would cut steps in half and drop figures, so parsing must be layout-aware and retrieval must return the whole procedure with its warnings (parent-child, step 05).
  6. List the failures that hurt and let code catch them. Wrong version, wrong number and missing warning are all checkable: filter superseded documents, verify that every number in the answer appears in the cited text, and always attach warning blocks verbatim. These checks are deterministic and cheaper and more reliable than asking a second model.
  7. Check the environment. Connectivity is unreliable, so a device bundle supports keyword and vector search over the technician's assigned equipment and shows cited passages without any generation.
  8. Test the obvious alternative with numbers. Putting whole manuals in a long context looks simple, but it costs about 22 times more at this volume (see capacity) and still risks the wrong version and missed details.

UC6 architecture: the answer path

One request, step by step

  1. The app sends the question with the work order. If there is a nameplate photo, OCR and a vision model extract model and serial, which are checked against the asset register.
  2. The resolver produces filters: model family, serial range, firmware, language and today's date for the effective-date check.
  3. Hybrid search returns about 50 keyword and 50 dense candidates, fused by reciprocal rank, then a cross-encoder reranks them (step 05).
  4. The top six hits expand to their parent procedures with the mandatory warnings attached.
  5. The model answers using only that context, quoting steps and values and naming the source page.
  6. The verifier checks every number, unit and part ID against the cited text and confirms that warnings are present and sources are current. A failure triggers one regeneration, then a refusal.

UC6 ingestion and freshness

  • Parsing quality caps answer quality. Measure parse accuracy on a sample of scanned and table-heavy pages before tuning retrieval.
  • Part numbers: normalise hyphens, spaces and case in both the index and the query, and keep the original string for display.
  • Figures: a vision model writes a short description and extracts labels once at ingest. If evaluation shows diagram questions still fail, add late-interaction page-image retrieval for those documents only, since storing multi-vector page embeddings for everything is expensive.
  • Language: never replace the source text with a translation; show the translation beside the cited original for safety content.

  • Latency budget: asset resolution 100 ms, retrieval and rerank 300 ms, first token about 1 second, verification 100 ms, which fits the 2-second target when online.

Use case 7: Catalog enrichment with a fine-tuned small model

ItemRequirement
BusinessMarketplace with 200,000 sellers and 60 million existing listings
TaskFrom a messy title and description, assign one of about 15,000 categories and extract attributes (brand, size, colour, material, compatibility, identifiers) into that category's schema
VolumeOne backfill of 60 million items, then about 1.5 million new or edited listings a day: roughly 17 a second on average and 60 a second at peak
LatencyNew listings enriched within about 5 minutes; no human waiting on a single item
QualityHigh precision on key attributes; output unknown rather than guess; every value traceable to a model version
ConstraintsSeller text may contain personal data; prohibited-item decisions are not delegated to this model
Where the scale isEverything the model needs is inside the listing itself. There is no private knowledge to retrieve, so the scale problem is cost, throughput and consistency across millions of near-identical calls.

UC7 decision path: how the approach was reached

  1. Decide whether retrieval belongs. The facts to extract are in the input, so RAG over documents adds nothing. Retrieval is used only to shortlist category candidates, because 15,000 labels cannot fit in a prompt.
  2. Start with the strongest model to learn what is possible. A frontier model with the schema and structured output is the quickest way to get good labels and to find the hard cases. This also acts as the teacher and as a fallback.
  3. Price the obvious design at real volume. Frontier-only costs about $35,000 for the backfill and about $26,500 a month afterwards (see capacity). The task is narrow and repetitive, which is the case where a small model fine-tuned on teacher labels can match quality at much lower cost (distillation, steps 07 and 12).
  4. Decide where quality is measured from. Teacher labels are noisy, so a separate human-labelled gold set is held out and never trained on. Gates are set on that gold set, not on agreement with the teacher.
  5. Make uncertainty a first-class output. The model abstains and reports confidence. Low-confidence items escalate to the teacher and then to people, so the small model only commits where it is reliable.
  6. Constrain the output. Schema-constrained decoding and validators catch malformed JSON, impossible units and invalid identifiers before they reach the catalog.
  7. Check the volume threshold. At 100,000 items a day the API would cost about $1,800 a month, so the frontier-only design would win on simplicity. Fine-tuning pays only above a volume where the savings exceed the cost of building and operating it.

UC7 architecture: build pipeline

  • Mix the data on purpose: stratify by category, seller, language and difficulty, and oversample rare categories and tricky attributes so the model does not only learn the common cases.
  • Guard against leakage: near-duplicate listings from one seller or product family must stay in one split, otherwise evaluation overstates quality.
  • Training compute is small: about 200,000 examples of roughly 820 tokens for two epochs is on the order of 20 to 40 GPU-hours on one modern GPU. The real cost is labelling, evaluation and operating the pipeline.

  • Mitigations: abstain on safety- or compatibility-critical attributes unless validated, retrain monthly on corrections, audit slices, and keep the previous model deployable.
