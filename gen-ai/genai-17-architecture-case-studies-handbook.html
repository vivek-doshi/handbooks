GenAI Architecture Case Studies: Enterprise Platform and Standards-Aligned Assistant

Steps 14 to 16 worked through five product use cases. These two case studies take a different angle: how to run GenAI as an enterprise platform that many teams share, and how to design one high-impact assistant against recognised risk-management and security standards.

How to reach an architecture

Neither design starts with a model or a framework. Each starts with the work being done, who can be harmed if it goes wrong, and what evidence the organisation needs. The same sequence applies to any GenAI system.

  1. Define the task and the cost of a wrong answer: who uses it, what decision it informs, and whether an error is cosmetic, expensive or harmful.
  2. Check that GenAI is needed: deterministic code, search or a classic model may be safer and cheaper. Use the decision ladder in step 12 and stop at the lowest rung that passes evaluation.
  3. Map data and permissions: sources, sensitivity, who may see what, retention, and whether identity must reach retrieval and tools.
  4. Tier the risk: read-only summarisation and customer-facing advice or actions need different controls. Match controls to impact rather than applying one gate to everything.
  5. Write the evaluation set first: golden questions, adversarial cases and unacceptable outcomes define done before any prompt is tuned.
  6. Place each control at the right boundary: gateway, retrieval, tool, output check, human review or release pipeline.
  7. Plan operation: traces, cost, incidents, model-version changes and the evidence retained for auditors and users.
Assumptions are part of the designEach case below states what it assumes. If your team size, risk, regulator or data differs, re-run the method instead of copying the result.

Use case 1: an enterprise GenAI platform

Context and requirements

A company with about 40 product teams wants GenAI features: summarisation, search, drafting and a few agents. Teams currently hold their own provider keys, build separate retrieval stacks and cannot answer basic questions about cost, data exposure or model versions. A central platform team can build shared services. Use cases range from low-risk internal summarisation to customer-facing workflows.

Reference flow

How the decisions were reached

  1. Centralise credentials and traffic first. The biggest uncontrolled risk is hundreds of keys and unknown data flows. A mandatory gateway gives one place for keys, redaction, routing, budgets and logs, and makes later controls enforceable instead of advisory.
  2. Make retrieval a shared product, not a shared index. Duplicate stacks multiply cost and ACL bugs, but one global index would weaken isolation. Teams share the service and its connectors while indexes stay scoped to tenant and data owner, with end-user identity propagated so retrieval respects permissions.
  3. Treat prompts and models as versioned releases. A registry with golden sets and CI evaluation turns prompt, model or retriever changes into reviewable changes with a regression signal, as step 10 recommends.
  4. Tier the risk. Internal summarisation of non-sensitive content can use the paved road with automated checks. Customer-facing output, regulated data or tool actions require design review, stronger evaluation and human approval. This keeps governance proportional.
  5. Allow multiple models behind one interface. A catalogue with tested, approved options and fallbacks avoids lock-in and supports routing by task, as in step 13. Do not assume equivalent names mean equivalent behaviour; evaluate before enabling a fallback.
  6. Make cost visible. Token budgets, tags and showback by team change behaviour more reliably than a policy document.

Alternatives and tradeoffs

AlternativeWhy not firstRevisit when
Each team integrates directly with a providerFastest start, but fragmented keys, controls, evidence and spendVery few teams and a single low-risk use case
One central team builds all GenAI featuresBecomes a bottleneck and lacks domain contextNeeds are narrow and uniform

Key risks: the platform becomes a single point of failure and a queue. Publish an SLO, provide break-glass and degraded modes, version the SDK, and measure adoption and developer experience, not only control coverage.

Use case 2: a standards-aligned assistant for a high-impact process

Context and requirements

An insurer wants an assistant that helps claims adjusters summarise a claim file, find relevant policy clauses and draft correspondence. The claim file contains sensitive personal and financial data, and an adjuster, not the model, makes the decision. Auditors, customers and possibly regulators may later ask how the assistant was designed, tested and monitored. The company has risk, security and compliance functions and a mandate to align with recognised frameworks.

Frameworks guide design; they do not certify itNIST AI RMF is a voluntary risk-management framework, with a Generative AI profile (NIST AI 600-1). The OWASP Top 10 for LLM Applications is a security risk list. ISO/IEC 42001 specifies an AI management system that an organisation may be audited against. The EU AI Act is law, not a design pattern, and applicability depends on the system's use and role. Confirm current versions and legal obligations with your risk, legal and compliance teams.
ReferenceHow it shapes the design
NIST AI RMF (Govern, Map, Measure, Manage)Gives the lifecycle structure: define context and impacts, measure with evidence, manage risks in operation, and govern roles and accountability

Reference flow

How the decisions were reached

  1. Map before design. Define intended use, excluded uses, affected customers, data classes and failure impact. The assistant drafts and summarises; it does not decide eligibility or payment. That scope decision removes the highest-impact failure from the model's authority.
  2. Choose the lowest rung that works. Per step 12, begin with access-controlled retrieval and a strong approved model, and add fine-tuning only if evaluation shows a specific gap. Retrieval gives current policy text and citations that auditors can inspect.
  3. Make permissions and privacy architectural. Retrieval is scoped to the case and the adjuster's rights. Prompts avoid unneeded personal data, vendor data-use terms are reviewed, and logging redacts or protects sensitive content with explicit retention.
  4. Threat-model with OWASP categories. Claim documents are untrusted input, so treat embedded instructions as data. Keep tools read-only, limit steps, require approval for any external message, and test prompt injection and data-exposure cases before release.
  5. Measure with evidence. Build an evaluation set from reviewed historical cases and failure modes. Track groundedness, citation validity, omissions, privacy leaks and escalation behaviour, and set release gates and acceptable thresholds with the risk owner rather than choosing them informally.
  6. Design human oversight to be real. Show sources and uncertainty, require review before use, record edits and rejections, and watch for automation bias. A review step that adjusters cannot realistically perform does not reduce risk.
  7. Operate as a managed system. Pin model versions, review changes through the same evaluation gate, monitor drift and complaints, keep an incident path, and map evidence to the management system. Revisit the design when the model, data, use or regulation changes.

Alternatives and tradeoffs

AlternativeWhy not firstRevisit when
Autonomous claim decisionsHigh impact and weak accountability for a probabilistic systemLaw, risk appetite, evaluation evidence and oversight design all support a narrower automated step

Key risks: compliance theatre, stale evidence, treating a framework mapping as proof of safety, and over-trusting reviewers. Test the controls and the people-and-process loop, not only the model.

Validation checklist

  • Can you show who can access each data source and prove retrieval honours the end user's permissions?
  • Does a change to a prompt, retriever, model or guardrail trigger evaluation before release?
  • Have injection, data-exfiltration, excessive-agency and fallback-model cases been tested?
  • Is the human decision point meaningful, and are accept, edit and reject outcomes recorded?
  • Can you reconstruct what the system saw, which model and version ran, and what the user did?
  • Are cost, latency, refusal, incident and drift signals monitored with owners and response paths?
  • Do legal, privacy and security owners agree on the current requirements, rather than assuming them from a framework?
PracticePick one GenAI feature you own. Classify its risk, list the decisions it influences, and draw the request path with the control at each boundary. Then write five evaluation cases, including two adversarial ones, and decide which control would catch each.
