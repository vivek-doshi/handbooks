Use Cases 8 and 9: Regulatory Research Assistant and Incident Response Agent

The first design is a RAG system where the hard problems are time and structure: which version of a rule was valid on a date, and which other articles it depends on. The second is an agent that investigates production incidents, where the hard problems are safety, untrusted telemetry and trust. Each case explains how the approach was reached.

How to read these designs

These cases use the same six questions as step 18: what a wrong answer is worth, where knowledge lives and how fast it changes, what the real inputs look like, what context is already known, which failures can code catch, and what it costs at real volume. The decision path in each case shows how those answers produced the design.

About the numbersAll figures are illustrative planning numbers. Prices assume $3 per million input tokens and $12 per million output tokens for a frontier reasoning model (output includes reasoning tokens), and $0.50 and $2.00 for a mid-size model. Replace them with measured token counts, current prices and benchmarks for your own stack.

Use case 8: Regulatory research assistant (RAG)

ItemRequirement
BusinessGlobal bank; about 1,500 compliance and legal staff covering 40 jurisdictions
ContentAbout 1.2 million documents (laws, regulations, regulator guidance, enforcement decisions, internal policies), roughly 15 million legal units, 14 languages
Change rateAbout 2,000 documents a day are new, amended or repealed; some amendments take effect on a future date
Questions“What are the reporting duties for X in country Y as of date D?”, cross-jurisdiction comparisons, “what changed since last quarter?”, definitions
VolumeAbout 18,000 questions a day: roughly 0.5 a second on average and 3 a second at peak; about 30 percent need multi-step research

RiskA wrong or outdated reading can cause a regulatory breach; queries are confidential and may be privileged
LatencySimple answers in about 15 seconds; research answers stream progress and may take up to 90 seconds
Where the difficulty isNot the volume or the model, but the shape of the content. Legal text is hierarchical, versioned over time and full of cross-references. A system that treats it as flat, undated chunks returns confident answers from the wrong version.

UC8 decision path: how the approach was reached

  1. Start from the users and the cost of error. Users are experts who put answers into memos for decisions with legal consequences. Fluency is worthless without verifiability, so the design must be evidence-first, cite precisely and prefer abstaining to guessing.
  2. Ask where the knowledge lives. It is external, changes daily and must be quoted. A fine-tuned model would be stale, cannot cite and cannot prove what it learned, so this is RAG, with the model used to read and synthesise rather than to remember.
  3. Look at the structure of the content. Legal documents already have a hierarchy and numbering (act, part, article, paragraph). Chunking by legal unit rather than by token count keeps each unit meaningful and makes the unit path (for example Reg X › Art 12 › (3)(b)) the citation itself.
  4. Look at the time dimension. The question usually has an as-of date, but similarity search ignores dates and returns the closest wording, whatever its validity. This is the most common source of confident, wrong legal answers. Validity is therefore modelled as data: each unit has valid-from and valid-to, amendments are new versions, and the as-of date is a required input that filters retrieval.
  5. Look at relationships. The answer often depends on a definition or a referenced article that does not resemble the question. We compared an LLM-extracted knowledge graph (GraphRAG, step 05) with a graph built from the citations that are already written in the text. Explicit citations are deterministic, auditable and free to build, while an LLM-built graph costs more and can invent edges. We use the citation graph and expand one or two hops at query time.
  6. Match effort to the question. Many questions are single lookups; others need decomposition by jurisdiction and issue. A router sends simple questions down a single-pass path and research questions into a bounded agentic loop of at most five retrieval rounds (step 09).
  7. Verify with code first. Each claim must carry a unit ID and a quote. Code checks that the quote appears verbatim in that unit and that the unit was valid on the as-of date. A second model checks entailment, and unsupported claims are removed or flagged.
  8. Treat confidentiality as architecture. Document-level access control, regional processing, no provider retention or training on queries, and an audit log are part of the design from the start.
  9. Price it per query. Research questions cost about ten times a simple lookup, so routing is the main cost lever (see capacity).

UC8 architecture: structure, time and citations

  • Amendments: keep every version of a unit and link them. “Repealed” or “replaced” becomes a validity end date, not a deletion, so questions about the past still work.
  • Parser quality: measure how often unit boundaries and cross-references are parsed correctly per source format and jurisdiction, because errors here silently corrupt citations.
  • Dates: store the document's published date as well as its effective period, since “as of” can mean either in practice. Ask the user when it matters.

UC8 architecture: answering and verifying

One research question, step by step

  1. The analyst asks, “What incident-reporting deadlines apply to a payment outage in country Y, as of 1 March?” The as-of date and jurisdiction become filters.
  2. The router classifies this as research. The planner decomposes it: which regulations apply, what counts as a reportable incident, and what the deadlines are.
  3. Each round runs hybrid search with the validity and jurisdiction filters, reranks, and expands the citation graph for definitions and referenced articles.
  4. The planner stops when each sub-question has supporting units or when five rounds are used, in which case the gap is reported.
  5. Synthesis writes claims, each with a unit ID and a quote. The verifier checks quotes, validity and entailment, and drops or flags what it cannot support.
  6. The memo shows the answer, pinpoint citations, any conflicts between sources, and the as-of date. The audit log records the run.

  cost = 40,000 * $3/M + 7,000 * $12/M = $0.120 + $0.084 = ~$0.204

Daily:   12,600 * $0.021 + 5,400 * $0.204 = $265 + $1,102 = ~$1,366  (~$41,000/month, ~$0.076 per question)
Levers:  route rewriting to a small model, cache the stable prompt prefix, cap rounds,
         answer repeated questions from reviewed memos with an as-of check

Index:   15 M units * 1024 dims
         float32 ~61 GB (SSD for rescoring); int8 ~15 GB, with HNSW overhead ~20 GB in RAM
         2 shards * 2 replicas of memory-optimised nodes; keyword index alongside
Rerank:  3 q/s * 100 candidates = ~300 pairs/s -> one GPU or a managed reranker
Ingest:  ~2,000 documents/day -> trivial embedding load; parse quality is the real cost
Avoided: LLM-written chunk prefixes on 15 M units ~ 10.5 B input tokens = ~$8,000 once, plus reruns on amendments
  • Cost sits in research mode: 30 percent of questions create about 80 percent of spend ($1,102 of $1,366), so a good router and a round cap matter more than the index size.
  • Cost is small against value: about $27 per user per month is low compared with researcher time, but the budget still needs a per-team limit and monitoring.

Use case 9: Incident response agent

ItemRequirement
BusinessPlatform with about 800 microservices, 60 engineering teams and 24/7 on-call
TaskWhen an incident pages, gather context, rank root-cause hypotheses with evidence, propose a mitigation and keep a timeline. People decide and approve
VolumeAbout 400 paged incidents a day after grouping; storms can produce 50 within five minutes
TimeFirst useful summary within two minutes of the page
DataMetrics, logs, traces, deploy history, feature flags, a service catalogue, and about 20,000 runbooks and postmortems (roughly 300,000 chunks)
SafetyNo uncontrolled production changes; telemetry may contain personal data and attacker-controlled text; the model never holds credentials

Where the difficulty isThe model is the easy part. The difficulty is giving it useful, small, trustworthy context from huge telemetry, and making sure it can never do harm, even when logs contain hostile instructions.

UC9 decision path: how the approach was reached

  1. Ask whether an agent is warranted. The first minutes are always the same: recent deploys, SLO burn, top error signatures, dependency health and similar past incidents. That is a fixed workflow and needs no model. The next step depends on what was found, which is an open-ended investigation and is where a loop earns its place (workflows versus agents, step 09). So the design is a deterministic context pack followed by a bounded agent.
  2. Ask what the worst failure is. A wrong or unsafe change in production, and hostile text in logs steering the model. The model therefore never executes anything. It proposes actions; a policy engine validates them; a person approves; deterministic automation runs only allow-listed actions within bounds.
  3. Do the context arithmetic. Raw logs for a busy service can be millions of lines. Code clusters errors into signatures, takes time-windowed samples and summarises them before the model sees anything, so each tool result is a few hundred tokens of evidence rather than a dump (context engineering, step 06).
  4. Place RAG where it belongs. Runbooks and postmortems are a modest knowledge base, so retrieval over them is one tool among several, not the architecture.
  5. Make claims checkable. Every hypothesis must cite the query and result that support it, and every query must pass programmatic limits (time window, service scope, row cap). The model may say it cannot determine a cause.
  6. Choose models by job. A reasoning model ranks hypotheses and plans investigation; a small model summarises logs. Compute the cost per run to confirm that it is small against the cost of an hour of outage.
  7. Earn trust in stages. Replay historical incidents, run in shadow mode, then assist in the incident channel, and only then enable a few approved, reversible actions.
  8. Operate the agent as a system. Trace every run, enforce budgets, keep a kill switch and protect the observability backend from query storms.

UC9 architecture: system flow

One incident, step by step

  1. Several alerts fire for the same cause. The correlator groups them and opens one incident, so one run starts, not twenty.
  2. The context pack runs in parallel with no model: deploys in the last two hours, SLO burn rate, top error signatures, dependency health and the three most similar past incidents. The result is about 6,000 tokens of facts.
  3. The agent reads the pack and decides what to check next. Each tool call goes through the gateway, which enforces time windows, service scope and row caps, redacts personal data and returns a compact result with a query ID.
  4. After at most eight steps, or when it has a supported answer, the agent posts ranked hypotheses with evidence links, what it ruled out, its confidence, and, if useful, a proposed action.
  5. A person may ask follow-up questions in the channel. To act, they approve the proposal; the policy engine checks blast radius and change freeze; the executor performs the action and logs it.
  6. After resolution, the agent drafts the timeline and postmortem outline from the recorded evidence for people to edit.

UC9 architecture: trust boundaries

UC9 capacity and cost

Runs: ~400 per day (one per grouped incident); storms: up to 50 in 5 minutes
Per run: context pack ~6,000 tokens; ~6 agent steps, each adding ~2,500 tokens of tool results
  input tokens ~ 6 * 6,000 + 2,500 * (0+1+2+3+4+5) = 36,000 + 37,500 = ~74,000
  output tokens ~ 6 * 600 (incl. reasoning) + 1,500 final report = ~5,100
  cost = 74,000 * $3/M + 5,100 * $12/M = $0.222 + $0.061 = ~$0.28 per run (before prefix-cache discounts)

Daily:   400 * $0.28 = ~$113  (~$3,400/month)
Storm:   50 runs * 74,000 = 3.7 M tokens in 3 minutes = ~20,000 input tokens/s
         -> cap concurrency at ~10 runs (~4,000 tokens/s) and queue the rest with a lightweight summary
Backend: ~30 queries per run * 400 = ~12,000 queries/day; protect the observability store with per-run quotas and a query cache
Runbook RAG: ~300,000 chunks -> a small index; not a scaling concern
  • Cost is not the constraint: about $0.28 a run is small next to an engineer-hour. Trust, safety and rate limits are the real constraints.
  • Latency budget: context pack under 30 seconds in parallel, agent steps of 10 to 15 seconds each, so a useful summary arrives in under two minutes with the first hypotheses posted early.

Patterns seen across steps 14 to 19

  • Use known context (asset, jurisdiction, as-of date, incident) to narrow the problem before any model call.
  • Put critical checks in code and use models for the parts that need language or judgement.
  • Give the system a way to say it does not know, and design what happens next.
  • Price the obvious alternative with real volume numbers before choosing a more complex design.
