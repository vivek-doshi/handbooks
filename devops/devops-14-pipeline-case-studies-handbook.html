DevOps pipeline architecture: two case studies

A pipeline is a production system: it handles source, credentials, artifacts, approvals, and deployment authority. These case studies show how an enterprise delivery model and a standards-informed regulated pipeline follow from their different organizational and assurance requirements.

1. How to reach a pipeline architecture

Do not start by choosing a CI vendor or copying a workflow. First establish what is being delivered, who operates it, what could go wrong, and what proof is needed. A "standard pipeline" is a set of reusable decisions and guardrails, not a workflow that ignores service risk.

  1. Define the service and its risk: users, data classification, deployment targets, dependencies, and impact of a bad release.
  2. Set delivery and reliability goals: required feedback speed, release cadence, recovery objectives, and availability of the pipeline itself.
  3. Map trust boundaries: contributors, CI identities, runners, registries, deployment controllers, cloud accounts, and approvers.
  4. Choose immutable promotion: build once, identify the artifact by digest, retain its test/security evidence, and promote that same artifact.
  5. Place controls at the right boundary: fast checks in CI, separation of duties for higher-risk changes, policy at deployment/admission, and monitoring after rollout.
  6. Design exceptions and recovery: named owner, rationale, expiry, audit trail, rollback/fix-forward path, and tested recovery from pipeline or platform failure.

Apply controls proportionately. A gate that cannot explain what risk it reduces becomes delay; a control that can be bypassed without evidence is not a dependable control.

2. Use case one: an enterprise delivery platform

Context and quality requirements

A company has dozens of product teams, hundreds of repositories, several cloud accounts, and both container and serverless workloads. Teams need autonomy to release independently, while security and operations require consistent identity, traceability, cost allocation, and production safeguards. A central platform team can operate shared CI templates and deployment services. Not every service has the same risk tier.

Team autonomyTeams own code, tests, service SLOs, and release outcomeFederated product ownership on a shared paved road

Reference flow

Why this approach fits

  1. Centralize the reusable parts, not every decision: platform-owned workflows standardize safe defaults; application teams still own tests and service-specific deployment behavior.
  2. Separate build authority from production authority: CI can publish artifacts and propose config changes, while a pull-based controller applies approved state using a scoped identity.
  3. Scale policy without copy-paste: organization rules require reviewed workflows and block unsafe permissions; versioned workflow changes use compatibility and rollout policies.
  4. Adjust controls by risk: production approval, isolation, evidence retention, and rollout strategy depend on service impact and data sensitivity; routine low-risk releases should not inherit unnecessary manual gates.
  5. Operate the pipeline as a service: publish an SLO, support model, recovery plan, runner isolation model, and adoption/feedback measures for the platform itself.

Tradeoff: a shared paved road reduces duplicated pipeline work but creates a high-impact platform dependency. Provide versioned interfaces, staged template rollouts, a supported break-glass path, and clear responsibility when either a workflow or application release fails.

3. Use case two: a standards-informed pipeline for regulated software

Context and assurance requirements

A healthcare claims service processes sensitive customer data and is delivered to multiple customer environments. Customers and auditors need evidence of secure development, artifact contents, build origin, approvals, and production deployment. The team must support vulnerability response and demonstrate that a deployed artifact is the one that passed its checks. The organization has a compliance program, security owner, and audit-evidence retention policy.

Standards are not interchangeableNIST SSDF is secure-development guidance; SLSA describes software supply-chain assurance; SPDX and CycloneDX describe SBOM formats; Sigstore and in-toto provide signing/attestation tooling and formats; OpenGitOps describes operating principles. They complement one another. Adopting them does not by itself certify a product or satisfy every regulatory obligation.
ReferenceRole in the pipelineWhat it does not prove by itself
NIST SSDF (SP 800-218)Organizes secure development practices across preparation, protection, production, and responseAutomatic compliance or a product security certification
SLSA provenanceRecords build inputs and process so consumers can verify artifact origin and build propertiesThat source code is safe or every business control was followed
SPDX or CycloneDX SBOMMachine-readable inventory supporting component identification and vulnerability responseAbsence of vulnerabilities, license compliance, or complete runtime inventory unless scope is defined
Sigstore / in-totoSupports artifact signing and verifiable statements about build or release stepsTrusted identity or policy unless verification roots, identities, and predicates are governed
OpenGitOps principlesVersioned desired state, pull-based reconciliation, declarative changes, and drift correctionSecurity of the repository, controller, or cluster without separate controls

Reference flow and evidence

How the design follows from assurance needs

  1. Turn guidance into owned controls: map the organization’s SSDF practices to pipeline jobs, repository settings, training, incident response, and named control owners. Keep the mapping and exceptions auditable.
  2. Bind evidence to the artifact: generate the SBOM and provenance from the actual build; sign the immutable digest; retain evidence in a governed store with access and retention rules.
  3. Verify independently at deploy time: admission or deployment policy checks the signature, trusted builder identity, expected source and provenance predicate, approved digest, and environment policy. A green CI run alone is not deployment authorization.
  4. Make release decisions risk-aware: approvals are tied to deployment context and separation-of-duties requirements. Automate routine low-risk releases where policy permits; require explicit review for defined high-risk changes.
  5. Design for vulnerability response: use SBOM data to identify affected deployed versions, rescan artifacts, notify owners, prioritize remediation, and preserve the incident/audit trail.

Important boundary: standards alignment is not a certification claim. Confirm the currently adopted version, customer/regulator requirements, threat model, evidence retention, and independent audit criteria with security, legal, compliance, and the authority responsible for the system.

4. Compare the approaches and validate

DimensionEnterprise paved roadStandards-informed regulated pipeline
Primary driverConsistent delivery and autonomy across many teamsTraceable assurance, evidence, and controlled changes
Shared foundationTemplates, identity, registry, policy, GitOpsSame foundation plus evidence schema, verification, retention, and control mapping
Approval modelRisk-tiered; avoid manual gates for every routine changeRisk- and obligation-based separation of duties and audit trail
Success measureTeam adoption, lead time, deployment stability, platform SLOEvidence completeness, verification outcomes, remediation time, release stability
Key riskShared platform bottleneck or unsafe exception pathCompliance theater, stale evidence, or treating a tool output as proof

Validation checklist

  • Can an operator trace a production deployment back to reviewed source, a build identity, and immutable artifact digest?
  • Are secrets and production credentials unavailable to untrusted pull-request code?
  • Can policy reject an invalid artifact before deployment, and is the rejection observable?
  • Are exceptions approved, scoped, logged, and time-bounded?
  • Have restore, rollback, runner compromise, registry outage, and credential revocation been exercised?
  • Do delivery metrics improve without hiding rework, outages, or unsafe bypasses?
PracticeChoose one service and create a control-to-evidence map. Draw its pull-request-to-production flow, identify who can change each trust boundary, and run a tabletop exercise for a compromised build runner and a critical dependency vulnerability.

Further reading

  • NIST SP 800-218: Secure Software Development Framework — secure software development practices; check NIST for revisions and drafts.
  • SLSA specifications — supply-chain levels and provenance requirements.
  • SPDX specifications — SBOM and software package data exchange.
  • CycloneDX specification — SBOM and related component metadata.
  • Sigstore documentation and in-toto — signing and verifiable supply-chain statements.
  • OpenGitOps — declarative, versioned, pull-based reconciliation principles.
  • DORA metrics guide — delivery performance measurement; not a compliance standard.
  • OpenTelemetry documentation — portable telemetry instrumentation and collection.
