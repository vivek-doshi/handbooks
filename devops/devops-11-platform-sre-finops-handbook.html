Step 11 · Advanced · Operate at scale: internal platforms, observability, reliability, and cost

Platform engineering, SRE and FinOps

At scale, every team solving the same problems separately is waste. Platform engineering offers self-service golden paths; SRE defines how reliable is reliable enough; FinOps keeps the bill understandable. This step joins them into one operating model.

1. Platform engineering

An internal developer platform (IDP) is a product built by a platform team for developers: self-service provisioning, golden paths for common service types, and guardrails built into the paved road. Typical parts: a developer portal (Backstage or similar), templates/scaffolding, CI/CD, GitOps, infrastructure APIs (Terraform modules or Crossplane), secrets, observability defaults, and policy.

  • Treat it as a product: users, roadmap, documentation, support, and feedback.
  • Make the golden path the easiest path, but not a cage; allow escape hatches with ownership.
  • Use the CNCF Platform Engineering Maturity Model (investment, adoption, interfaces, operations, measurement; levels from provisional to optimizing) to assess where you are.
  • Measure with DORA metrics plus developer-experience signals (time to first deploy, onboarding time, satisfaction).

2. Observability with OpenTelemetry

Observability asks "why is it behaving this way?" using metrics, logs, and traces that share context. OpenTelemetry (OTel) is the vendor-neutral standard for generating and shipping them: instrument with SDKs or auto-instrumentation, send OTLP to the OpenTelemetry Collector, and route to backends (Prometheus, Grafana stack, Tempo, Loki, or commercial tools).

  • Follow semantic conventions for names and attributes so telemetry correlates across services.
  • Use tail or probabilistic sampling to control trace volume and cost; keep metric label cardinality bounded.
  • Alert on symptoms users feel (SLOs), not on every cause. Keep dashboards for the golden signals: latency, traffic, errors, saturation.

3. SLOs, error budgets and alerting

  1. Choose SLIs that reflect user experience (availability, latency, correctness).
  2. Set SLO targets per service based on user need and cost, not 100%.
  3. The remainder, 1 − SLO, is the error budget. If it is burning too fast, slow feature releases and invest in reliability.
  4. Use multi-window burn-rate alerts: page on fast burn (for example 14× over 1 hour and 5 minutes), ticket on slow burn.

4. Incidents and disaster recovery

  • Define severities, on-call rotations, and roles (incident commander, communications, operations).
  • Run the loop: acknowledge, assemble, triage, mitigate (roll back or fail over first), resolve, then a blameless post-incident review with tracked actions.
  • Keep runbooks next to alerts and test them in game days.
  • Set RTO and RPO per tier, back up clusters, databases, and state; practice restores (including point-in-time database recovery). An untested backup is a hope, not a control.

5. FinOps: cost as an engineering metric

FinOps is the practice of bringing financial accountability to cloud spend through three loops: inform (allocation and visibility), optimize (rightsizing, commitments, spot, autoscaling, scheduling non-prod off), and operate (governance and review cadence).

  • Enforce required tags/labels (owner, team, environment, cost center) through policy at admission and in Terraform.
  • Allocate shared cluster cost per namespace or team (OpenCost or cloud tooling); use FOCUS-formatted billing data to compare across providers.
  • Put cost checks in pipelines (Infracost) and review reserved/committed capacity quarterly.
  • Include cost and sustainability in architecture reviews, alongside reliability and security.
PracticeFor one service, define two SLOs, implement burn-rate alerts, write a runbook for the top alert, and produce a monthly cost report broken down by team label.
