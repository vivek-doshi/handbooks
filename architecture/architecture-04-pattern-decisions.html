Step 04 · Beginner · Decision maker's guide

Choose architecture patterns by evidence

Architectural patterns are bundles of tradeoffs, not maturity milestones or badges of honor. This lesson compares common structural patterns and provides a rigorous decision framework to match designs to real business requirements, team topologies, and operational realities.

Monolith to Microservices Event-Driven & Sagas Serverless Tradeoffs Strangler Fig Migrations Total Cost Modeling

1. The Evidence-Based Decision Framework

Never pick an architecture pattern before articulating the exact business problem and technical constraints. Ask these 5 critical diagnostic questions:

  • Team Independence: How many autonomous engineering teams exist today, and do they suffer from release-train blocking, merge conflicts, and coordination delays?
  • Data Consistency: Does the domain strictly require immediate ACID transactions, or can business workflows tolerate eventual consistency (seconds to minutes)?
  • Independent Scaling Dynamics: Does one specific component experience 100x the load or memory requirements of the rest of the application?
  • Operational Maturity: Does the organization possess 24/7 SRE on-call support, automated CI/CD canary deployments, distributed tracing, and centralized log aggregation?
  • Reversibility & Migration Cost: If the chosen pattern proves ill-suited in 12 months, what is the engineering cost and timeline to revert or migrate?

2. Monoliths and Modular Monoliths

A monolith compiles and deploys as a single unit. It remains the gold standard for early-stage products, small teams, and domains where data boundaries are still emerging.

Monolith Scaling and Deployment Strategies

  • Horizontal Scaling: Run multiple stateless instances behind an L7 load balancer (Round Robin or Least Connections). Session state is offloaded to a Redis cluster or stored in signed JWT client cookies.
  • Database Read Replicas: Route read-heavy queries (search, reporting) to read-only database replicas while directing writes to a primary database.
  • Zero-Downtime Blue-Green / Rolling Deployments: Deploy new container versions incrementally, draining connections from old pods only after health probes succeed.

When Monoliths Break Down

A monolith breaks down not because of traffic (Shopify handles over $5B in Black Friday sales on a modular Ruby on Rails monolith), but because of organizational scaling. When 50+ developers commit to a single repository without boundary enforcement, build times explode, flaky tests block releases, and one developer's bug crash impacts the entire application.

3. Microservices: The Autonomy and Complexity Tax

Microservices trade in-process simplicity for organizational autonomy. A microservice is a boundary of independent business capability, deployment, and data ownership.

Decomposition Strategies

  • By Business Capability: Group services around organizational functions (e.g., Billing, Inventory Management, Customer Support).
  • By Domain-Driven Design (DDD) Bounded Context: Define explicit linguistic and semantic boundaries. The concept of an "Account" in Sales differs fundamentally from an "Account" in Ledger; each belongs in its own bounded context.
  • Inverse Conway Maneuver: Organize architecture boundaries to reflect the desired independent team structure (e.g., Team A owns Catalog, Team B owns Checkout).

The Microservice Readiness Checklist

  1. Can every service be deployed independently to production without deploying any other service?
  2. Does every service strictly own its private database, with zero direct database queries from external services?
  3. Is distributed tracing (OpenTelemetry/Jaeger) instrumented across all service network hops?
  4. Are all inter-service network calls protected by timeouts, circuit breakers, and bounded retries?
  5. Is there an automated contract-testing pipeline (Pact) preventing breaking API changes?
  6. Does each service have dedicated on-call alerts, dashboards, and an accountable owning team?

4. Event-Driven Architecture (EDA) and Sagas

In an event-driven system, producers broadcast immutable events representing state changes (e.g., OrderPlaced), and independent consumers react asynchronously.

Choreography vs. Orchestration

DimensionChoreography (Decentralized)Orchestration (Centralized)
How it Works Services listen to events and autonomously decide what to do next without a central controller. A central workflow orchestrator (e.g., Temporal, AWS Step Functions) sends explicit commands to services.
Strengths High decoupling, simple setup for 2–3 step flows, rapid independent evolution. Clear centralized state visibility, easy timeout handling, simplified debugging and retry control.
Weaknesses "Pinball machine" architecture; hard to trace end-to-end workflow state; cyclic dependencies. Orchestrator can become a single point of failure or centralized business logic bottleneck.
When to Use Simple notifications, broadcast fan-outs, independent analytics consumers. Multi-step transactional business processes (e.g., checkout, loan approval, booking).

The Saga Pattern: Distributed Transactions Without 2PC

When an operation spans multiple services (Order Service, Payment Service, Inventory Service), traditional distributed two-phase commits (2PC) cause severe lock contention and availability fragility. A Saga coordinates a sequence of local transactions, executing compensating transactions if a step fails:

Order Saga Execution Flow:
  Step 1: Order Service creates Pending Order.
  Step 2: Payment Service authorizes card. (SUCCESS)
  Step 3: Inventory Service reserves stock. (FAILS - Out of Stock!)
  Compensating Action 3a: Payment Service refunds authorized charge.
  Compensating Action 3b: Order Service marks Order as Canceled (Out of Stock).
  Notification: Notify customer of cancellation with clear reason.

5. Serverless and Function-as-a-Service (FaaS)

Serverless shifts operational provisioning, auto-scaling, and OS patching to the cloud provider. You pay strictly for execution time and memory consumed.

Great Serverless CandidatesPoor Serverless Candidates
Event-Driven Processing: Responding to S3 object uploads, SQS message queues, webhook ingest. Long-Running Compute: Heavy data processing, video rendering, machine learning model training (> 15 min limits).
Bursty, Unpredictable Traffic: E-commerce flash sales, emergency notification dispatchers. Constant High-Throughput Baseline: Steady 24/7 web API traffic (FaaS becomes 3x–5x more expensive than containerized VMs).
Scheduled Batch Jobs: Nightly database cleanup, automated report generation. Low-Latency Services with Cold Starts: User-facing latency-critical APIs sensitive to 500ms+ initialization spikes.

Cold Start Mitigation Strategies

  • Language Selection: Go, Rust, or Node.js start in 20–50 ms; Java and .NET traditionally take 500–2,000 ms unless compiled ahead-of-time (GraalVM Native Image, .NET Native AOT).
  • Provisioned Concurrency: Keep a warm pool of pre-initialized execution environments during peak operational hours.
  • Lightweight Bundles: Minimize deployment artifact size, strip unused dependencies, and avoid heavy ORMs that scan classpaths at startup.

6. Hybrid Architectures and Strangler Fig Migrations

Real-world enterprise systems are rarely pure monoliths or pure microservices; they are hybrid topologies. When migrating away from a legacy system, never attempt a "big-bang" rewrite.

The 5-Stage Strangler Fig Migration Pattern

  1. Intercept: Place an API Gateway or Reverse Proxy (Envoy, Nginx, AWS API Gateway) in front of the existing legacy monolith. All traffic flows through the gateway.
  2. Carve Out Boundary: Select a single, high-value, bounded domain module (e.g., Notifications or Customer Reviews). Build the new microservice independently.
  3. Shadow / Dual-Write: Route traffic to both the legacy monolith and the new service. Compare outputs, latency, and correctness in background logs without user impact.
  4. Cut Over: Reroute production traffic for that specific route to the new microservice. Keep the legacy code as a fast rollback option.
  5. Prune: Delete the deprecated legacy module code and database tables once the new service operates stably in production for 30 days. Repeat for the next module.

7. CQRS and Event Sourcing: When Are They Justified?

Command Query Responsibility Segregation (CQRS) separates write operations (Commands that mutate state and enforce business invariants) from read operations (Queries optimized for fast UI presentation).

The CQRS / Event Sourcing Reality Check

Do you need CQRS? Only if:

  • Read traffic outnumbers write traffic by > 50:1 and requires complex aggregations joining 10+ tables.
  • Write models have complex, highly contested domain validation logic, while reads need denormalized flat views.
  • You have the engineering maturity to handle eventual consistency between writes and read projections.
Do you need Event Sourcing? Only if:
  • Complete historical auditability and temporal queries ("What was the state on Nov 3rd at 2:15 PM?") are legal or core business requirements (e.g., banking ledgers, supply chain tracking).
  • You are prepared to manage schema evolution, event upcasters, snapshots, and GDPR cryptographic deletion.
If not, a standard relational database with optimized read replicas is simpler, cheaper, and faster to deliver.

8. Comprehensive Pattern Decision Matrix

Scenario / Business ContextRecommended Architecture PatternKey Tradeoffs to AcceptRe-Evaluate When
Early-stage startup / MVP, 1-2 teams, rapid pivoting Modular Monolith Single point of failure; shared release cycle. Team grows past 25 engineers or distinct workloads require 10x resource isolation.
High-traffic multi-tenant B2B SaaS platform Modular Core + Targeted Worker Services Operational overhead for worker pipelines. Specific enterprise tenants demand dedicated physical data sovereignty.
Real-time IoT telemetry ingestion (100k events/sec) Event-Driven Ingestion Pipeline (Kafka + Stream Processor) Eventual consistency; out-of-order event handling. Ingestion volume drops or shifts to synchronous command APIs.
Bursty, occasional batch workflows (invoice generation) Serverless Functions (FaaS) Cold start latencies; vendor platform constraints. Workload becomes 24/7 continuous, causing FaaS billing to exceed VM costs.
Enterprise legacy modernization (15-year mainframe/ERP) Strangler Fig with API Gateway Facade Dual maintenance of legacy and modern systems during multi-year transition. All business capabilities successfully migrated to modern target architecture.

10. Practical Exercises

  1. Architecture Selection Defense: A mobility startup is building an Uber-like ride-hailing app with 6 developers. The CTO proposes launching immediately with 18 microservices deployed on Kubernetes with Kafka. Prepare a 1-page counter-proposal defending a Modular Monolith, detailing the operational complexity avoided and defining the exact metrics that would justify extracting services later.
  2. Saga Design Exercise: Design an e-commerce checkout saga covering Order Placement, Payment Authorization, Loyalty Points Deduction, and Inventory Allocation. Write the sequence of forward events and the exact compensating rollback steps if the Loyalty Points service fails.
  3. Strangler Fig Planning: A legacy monolithic insurance platform handles policy quoting, claims, and billing in a single Oracle database. Create a phased 4-quarter Strangler Fig migration plan to extract the Claims module, including data synchronization and rollback mechanisms.
