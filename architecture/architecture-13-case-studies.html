Architecture case studies: from use case to design

There is no universally "good" architecture. A good design fits the business outcome, quality requirements, constraints, and operating capability—and makes its important tradeoffs and failure behavior explicit. These two examples show how to get from a use case to a defensible design.

1. A repeatable way to reach an architecture

Start with the decision the system must support, not a preferred technology or diagram. The solution below each case is good only under the stated assumptions; change the workload, risk, team, or business constraint and the design may change.

  1. Frame the use case: name the users, trigger, desired outcome, and system boundary.
  2. Make quality needs concrete: prioritize correctness, latency, throughput, availability, recovery, privacy, and cost. Use measurable targets when stakeholders can supply them.
  3. Write invariants and constraints: identify what must never happen, data owners, compliance needs, existing systems, team skills, and delivery limits.
  4. Trace the normal and failure flows: show synchronous calls, durable state, retries, timeouts, duplicates, and recovery.
  5. Compare the simplest viable options: state why an option fits, what it costs, and what evidence would justify a different one.
  6. Validate and revisit: test risky assumptions with a prototype, load test, threat review, operational exercise, or production evidence.
Useful design testCan another engineer explain who owns each piece of data, what happens when a dependency fails, how the system recovers, and which assumption would trigger a redesign?

These are architecture examples, not blueprints. Do not copy their components without checking whether the requirements that justify them exist in your own system.

2. Use case one: place an order when payment can be slow

Problem and design drivers

A customer checks out with several items. The system must prevent duplicate orders when a customer retries, must never mark an order paid without provider confirmation, and should let the customer see progress if payment takes time. A payment provider can time out or be temporarily unavailable. Assume one product team, moderate initial traffic, and no requirement for every domain to deploy independently.

ConcernRequirement / invariantArchitecture consequence
Order correctnessOne logical order per checkout idempotency keyPersist the key with a uniqueness constraint and return the prior result on retry
Payment truthOnly confirmed provider outcomes change payment stateModel pending, authorized, failed, and unknown outcomes explicitly; reconcile uncertainty
Provider outageDo not lose an accepted order or hold a request open indefinitelyPersist order intent, process payment asynchronously, and expose order status
Team and operationsSmall team; avoid unnecessary distributed operationsStart with a modular application and a durable outbox, not a fleet of microservices

Chosen flow

How the design follows from the requirements

  1. Begin with correctness: order state and the outbox event are committed together, so a saved order cannot silently lose its payment work. A unique idempotency key makes a repeated checkout request return the original order.
  2. Decouple the unreliable boundary: payment is asynchronous because the provider is outside the system's control. The API can acknowledge a pending order without pretending payment succeeded.
  3. Keep operations proportional: order and payment modules share one deployable and database initially. The outbox and worker add only the async mechanism required for reliable handoff.
  4. Make uncertainty visible: a timeout is not proof that a charge failed. Use provider idempotency where supported, persist attempt identifiers, process callbacks idempotently, and reconcile unknown outcomes before retrying a charge.
  5. Protect the customer experience: expose explicit states such as pending_payment, paid, payment_failed, and review_required; do not use a generic success status for unresolved payment.
Alternative consideredWhy not firstRevisit when
Synchronous checkout calls the payment provider before saving the orderProvider latency/outages lengthen checkout and can leave the outcome ambiguous after a timeoutThe provider has a dependable latency/availability contract and the business requires an immediate decision
Separate microservice for every moduleIndependent deployment is not a stated need; it adds network, deployment, and on-call failure modesTeams, scale, isolation, or release requirements demonstrate real independent ownership
Distributed transaction across order DB, broker, and providerThese systems do not share one practical atomic transaction; model the workflow with durable state and idempotent steps insteadNot a normal revisit; reconsider the business workflow or provider contract if its invariants cannot be met

Validate before launch: test duplicate checkout, relay restarts, duplicate/out-of-order messages, provider timeouts after acceptance, invalid callbacks, and reconciliation. Alert on old pending orders and outbox/consumer lag. Define refund/compensation and manual review ownership.

3. Use case two: live product analytics for many tenants

Problem and design drivers

Many customer applications send usage events. Product teams need near-real-time dashboards and longer-term analysis. Traffic arrives in bursts, one event may be delivered more than once, and analytics can tolerate a short delay. Assume events are append-oriented, dashboard reads are much more common than corrections, and losing a small number of events is unacceptable.

ConcernRequirement / invariantArchitecture consequence
Ingestion burstsAbsorb spikes without making every producer wait for aggregationAccept and durably buffer events before stream processing
DeliveryDuplicates are possible; accepted events must be recoverableStable event IDs, at-least-once delivery, idempotent aggregation, replayable retention
FreshnessDashboards can lag briefly but need predictable updatesStream aggregates with an explicit freshness/SLO target and visible lag
Privacy and costTenant separation, bounded retention, and controlled query costAuthenticate producers, minimize payloads, apply tenant-aware access and retention policies

Chosen flow

How the design follows from the requirements

  1. Buffer bursts: the ingestion API validates identity, schema, size, and quota, then acknowledges only after the configured durable-acceptance condition is met. Stream processing can scale separately from producers.
  2. Preserve recoverability: retain an immutable event envelope with a stable event ID, event time, schema version, tenant, and trace context. Choose partition keys to balance tenant isolation, ordering needs, and hot-partition risk.
  3. Handle duplicates and poison records: assume at-least-once delivery; make aggregate updates idempotent or deduplicate by event ID. Bound retries, quarantine invalid events, alert, and provide controlled replay.
  4. Serve the actual read pattern: store raw governed events for permitted replay/analysis and materialize dashboard aggregates for fast queries. Make freshness and late-event correction behavior visible.
  5. Protect tenants and data: authenticate producers, enforce tenant scoping at ingestion and query time, minimize sensitive payloads, and define deletion, retention, encryption, and audit controls.
Alternative consideredWhy not firstRevisit when
Write each event synchronously to a dashboard databaseCouples ingestion availability and burst handling to query-store capacity; raw-event replay is harderTraffic is small and stable, and the simpler design meets measured freshness and recovery needs
Use a globally ordered streamGlobal ordering limits parallelism and usually exceeds the business requirementA documented invariant truly requires total ordering across all tenants and events
Build a large streaming platform immediatelyOperational cost and specialized on-call ownership may exceed current valueMeasured throughput, retention, replay, or latency requirements exceed a managed/simple queue design

Validate before launch: load-test bursts and large tenants; test broker outages, lag, duplicate and late events, schema changes, replay, and dead-letter growth. Set explicit ingestion durability, dashboard freshness, retention, and recovery targets. Do not claim exactly-once business outcomes from a broker guarantee alone.

Architecture review questions

  • Which requirement or invariant justifies each major component?
  • What is the source of truth, and who owns each state transition?
  • What is acknowledged to a caller, and when is work actually durable?
  • What happens on timeout, duplicate delivery, partial failure, and replay?
  • How are authentication, tenant isolation, privacy, retention, and audit enforced?
  • Can the team operate the system, detect degradation, and recover within its goals?
  • What measurable evidence would justify the next architectural step?
PracticeChoose a use case from your own work. Write three quality scenarios and two invariants, draw the normal and failure flows, compare a simple design with one alternative, then ask a reviewer to challenge each component that has no stated reason to exist.
