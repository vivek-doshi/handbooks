Step 02 · Beginner · Enterprise context

Enterprise architecture, made practical

Enterprise architecture connects business outcomes to the applications, data, technology, security, and integrations that deliver them. The goal is useful alignment and managed change—not a giant diagram collection or a gate that slows every team.

Six domainsGovernanceTechnology choicesADRs and C4

1. The six architecture domains

Organizations group architecture work in different ways. This practical six-domain view is a learning aid, not a claim that every framework uses the same taxonomy.

DomainQuestion it answersUseful artifacts
BusinessWhat capabilities, outcomes, and value streams matter?Capability map, value stream, outcome measures
ApplicationWhich applications support those capabilities and where are boundaries?Application landscape, context and container diagrams
DataWhat information is authoritative, sensitive, retained, and shared?Data ownership map, flows, classification, lineage
TechnologyWhat runtime, network, platform, and infrastructure are needed?Deployment view, standards, platform capabilities
SecurityHow are identities, threats, privacy, and controls handled?Threat model, trust boundaries, control mapping
IntegrationHow do systems exchange commands, queries, and events?API/event contracts, dependency map, integration patterns
Start from outcomesTrace each important technology choice to a business capability, a quality requirement, or a risk reduction. If none applies, challenge whether the choice is needed.

2. Selecting technology: build, buy, integrate

Evaluate capabilities before vendor names. Score options against functional fit, security and privacy, interoperability, reliability, accessibility, skills, migration cost, exit cost, support, and total cost of ownership.

Build vs. buy vs. integrate

  • Build when the capability differentiates the product, the organization can operate it, and the lifecycle cost is understood.
  • Buy when a maintained product meets the requirements better than a bespoke implementation and contractual risks are acceptable.
  • Integrate when existing systems are valuable but need a controlled contract, adapter, or shared workflow.

Include data portability, identity integration, auditability, regional availability, service-level commitments, renewal terms, and a tested exit plan. A proof of concept should answer a named uncertainty with a time limit and measurable pass/fail criteria; it is not a miniature production build.

Technology radar and standards

Maintain a living radar (adopt, trial, assess, hold) with owners and rationale. Treat it as guidance, not a substitute for a context-specific exception. Technology and coding standards should state a default, the problem it solves, the supported version policy, and how teams request an exception. Enforce objective rules with formatters, linters, dependency checks, CI policy, and architecture tests; keep human review for context-dependent judgment. Give every standard an owner and review trigger so it remains living guidance rather than a stale gate.

3. Reviews, governance, and roles

An Architecture Review Board (ARB) should focus on cross-cutting impact: regulatory commitments, shared platforms, major risks, interoperability, and decisions that are costly to reverse. Delegate local choices to the teams closest to the work.

  • Architecture review: test options and tradeoffs early; publish decisions and owners.
  • Design review: inspect a concrete change against requirements and interfaces.
  • Code review: validate implementation, maintainability, tests, and security findings.
  • Governance gates: automate objective checks where possible; use human review for contextual risks.

Solution architects translate business constraints into an implementable design and work with developers, operations, security, data owners, product managers, and procurement. Responsibility is collaborative: architecture does not end at a diagram handoff.

Common governance failures

Avoid reviews that happen after design is fixed, standards with no ownership, mandatory approvals for low-risk changes, and resume-driven development (choosing fashionable tools without a user need). Make exceptions visible, time-bounded, and reversible where practical.

4. Standards and documentation that stay useful

Documentation is a product for its readers. Give each artifact an owner, purpose, audience, update trigger, and source of truth. Prefer small diagrams linked to maintained code and deployment metadata over duplicated inventories.

Typical documentation types include principles and standards, ADRs, context/container diagrams, data-flow and sequence diagrams, interface contracts, operational runbooks, and migration roadmaps. Store them where their intended readers work, link related artifacts, and retire or label superseded material instead of leaving conflicting copies active.

A minimal ADR

# Decision: Use a managed message broker
Status: Accepted
Context: Order processing must survive downstream outages.
Decision: Publish durable order events through the managed broker.
Alternatives: Synchronous calls; self-hosted broker.
Consequences: Eventual consistency, replay controls, and broker cost.

5. Worked enterprise example

For an order platform, map the business capability (order fulfillment) to applications (storefront, order service, warehouse system), authoritative data (order record and inventory owner), technology (runtime and broker), security (customer identity and staff roles), and integrations (payment provider and shipment events).

  1. Name the customer outcome and constraints.
  2. Find the system and data owners; mark sensitive information and trust boundaries.
  3. Record current state, target state, transition steps, and affected teams.
  4. Choose a small experiment for uncertain parts and define success measures.
  5. Capture the decision and review date; automate repeatable policy checks.
PracticeChoose a SaaS capability your organization could buy. Compare building, buying, and integrating it. Include a data-exit test, security review, total-cost estimate, and one reason to revisit the decision.
