TOGAF: structure enterprise change

TOGAF provides a configurable enterprise-architecture method, content, and governance vocabulary. It helps organizations make change coherent across business, data, application, and technology concerns—but should be tailored to the decisions and outcomes the organization actually needs.

TOGAF Standard 10th EditionADM cycleGovernance and repositoryAdapt, do not over-process

1. What TOGAF is, its purpose, and version context

The Open Group's TOGAF Standard 10th Edition combines enduring fundamental content with series guides and practice guidance. It is designed to be configured for different organizations and use cases; it is not a required waterfall lifecycle or a prescribed set of documents for every project.

TOGAF has evolved through multiple editions; verify the edition and applicable series guides used by your organization rather than mixing older terminology with current practice. The 10th Edition deliberately separates stable fundamental content from evolving, topical guidance, so check the official library when citing a specific method or artifact.

Use it when an organization needs shared architecture practices, cross-domain alignment, repeatable change governance, or a common vocabulary across a portfolio. A small product team may need only selected practices. Compare with other methods based on outcomes, governance, modeling needs, and team capability rather than framework branding.

2. Framework structure and the Architecture Development Method

The Architecture Development Method (ADM) is iterative. It starts with preliminary organization and principles, frames an architecture vision, develops business/data/application/technology architectures, plans migration, governs implementation, and manages change. Requirements management continues throughout.

ADM phasePractical intent
PreliminaryConfigure architecture capability, principles, governance, and scope
A: Architecture VisionSet vision, stakeholders, scope, value, and high-level risks
B: Business ArchitectureDescribe capabilities, processes, organization, and target outcomes
C: Information SystemsDevelop data and application views and their relationships
D: Technology ArchitectureDefine technology capabilities and target platform landscape
E: Opportunities and SolutionsGroup work into solution options and transition architectures
F: Migration PlanningPrioritize work, dependencies, cost, risk, and roadmap
G: Implementation GovernanceConfirm delivery remains aligned and manage deviations
H: Architecture Change ManagementMonitor change triggers and decide when architecture must evolve

Iteration is normal: a project may revisit phases as requirements, funding, risk, and implementation evidence change.

3. Deliverables, artifacts, and the BDAT domains

Choose deliverables by stakeholder decision. Common outputs include an architecture vision, baseline and target views, gap analysis, requirements, risk register, transition architectures, implementation roadmap, and governance records. An artifact is useful only when a stakeholder can act on it.

  • Business: capabilities, value streams, processes, and outcomes.
  • Data: data ownership, concepts, movement, quality, and governance.
  • Application: applications, services, interactions, and responsibilities.
  • Technology: infrastructure, platforms, networks, and deployment capabilities.

Security, integration, and operations are cross-cutting concerns that must appear in relevant views and decisions. TOGAF artifacts can coexist with C4 diagrams, threat models, ADRs, product roadmaps, and agile backlogs.

4. Architecture repository and capability

An architecture repository organizes reusable standards, reference architectures, solution building blocks, models, decisions, and work products. Define owners, versioning, access, quality, lifecycle state, and review date. Do not create a repository of stale files with no link to delivery work.

An architecture capability includes people and skills, governance, methods, tools, content, and funding. Establish sponsorship, decision rights, architecture roles, and a process for exceptions. Measure whether the capability improves coherent delivery and reduces material risk—not the number of templates completed.

5. Governance, roles, and stakeholder collaboration

Governance sets decision rights, guardrails, assurance, and escalation paths. A board should focus on cross-portfolio or high-risk choices; teams should retain authority over local reversible choices within agreed principles.

  • Enterprise architect: aligns portfolio change and enterprise-wide principles.
  • Domain / solution architect: shapes coherent designs for a domain or initiative.
  • Delivery teams: own implementation, operability, tests, and feedback from production.
  • Business, security, data, and operations stakeholders: validate outcomes, constraints, risk, and controls.

Use architecture contracts and checkpoints to surface deviations early. Keep waivers time-bounded and track residual risk. Integrate with agile product planning by slicing architecture work into decisions and enabling capabilities, not a large upfront blueprint disconnected from delivery.

6. A practical adoption approach and common pitfalls

  1. Identify an enterprise change problem and stakeholders who feel its impact.
  2. Configure only the ADM, views, and governance needed to address it.
  3. Set principles, ownership, repository practices, and a lightweight decision record.
  4. Map baseline and target states, gaps, dependencies, and transition steps.
  5. Integrate reviews with delivery planning and automate objective controls.
  6. Measure outcomes, gather feedback, and remove process that adds no value.

Common pitfalls include treating ADM as a strictly linear process, producing every artifact regardless of audience, confusing compliance with architecture quality, centralizing every decision, and using TOGAF terms without connecting them to implementation. Avoid claiming TOGAF is a product architecture or a vendor-specific tool.

PracticeChoose a legacy application replacement. Sketch the vision and scope, map business/data/application/technology impacts, identify one transition architecture, and name the governance decision that must occur before implementation.
