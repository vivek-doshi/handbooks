Cloud architecture by example: an enterprise AI platform

The previous book used AI inside systems. This capstone treats AI as the system: a shared platform where models, data, GPUs and governance are the core design problems.

LLM gatewayRetrieval serviceTraining platformGPU inferenceEvaluationGovernance

What changes when AI is the product

  • Capacity and utilisation of scarce accelerators dominate cost and reliability.
  • Evaluation and governance become platform capabilities that every team relies on.
  • Data, models and prompts are versioned assets with lineage.
  • Security extends to model weights, training data and the supply chain of model artefacts.

Use case 6: Enterprise generative AI platform with training and inference

A company wants a shared AI platform: a governed LLM gateway for all teams, a retrieval service, fine-tuning of open models on private data, and scalable GPU inference for latency-sensitive and cost-sensitive workloads. AI is the product here, not a feature.

Requirements

  • Serve many applications through one governed entry point with quotas and showback
  • Support hosted frontier models and self-hosted open models
  • Fine-tune models on private data inside a secure boundary
  • Inference p95 under 2 seconds for chat; throughput and cost targets for batch
  • Reproducible training, evaluation gates and model lineage

Architecture flow

  1. Applications call an AI gateway that authenticates, applies policy and quotas, redacts sensitive data, caches, logs and routes to a model.
  2. Routing picks between hosted model APIs and self-hosted endpoints by task, data sensitivity, latency and cost; fallbacks cover outages and quota limits.
  3. A shared retrieval service manages ingestion, embeddings, vector indexes and access control for all applications.
  4. A training platform provisions GPU or accelerator clusters, runs data preparation, fine-tuning and evaluation pipelines, and writes models to a registry with lineage.
  5. Approved models deploy to inference services with autoscaling, batching and optimised runtimes (for example vLLM or TensorRT-LLM), on GPUs or custom accelerators.
  6. A platform layer provides evaluation, red-team suites, observability, cost showback and a catalogue of approved models and prompts.

Where AI fits

  • Evaluation is the quality gate: automated test suites, human review, safety and bias tests, and regression checks before any model reaches production.
  • Observe quality in production (feedback, groundedness, drift) and loop it back into data and training.

Security and governance

  • Data classification drives routing: restricted data uses only private or self-hosted models in approved regions.
  • Isolated training environments with no internet access, private registries, signed model artefacts and scanned dependencies.
  • Protect model weights as crown-jewel assets: encryption, access logging, confidential computing where needed.
  • Policies for acceptable use, human oversight, transparency and risk tiering aligned to the EU AI Act and NIST AI RMF.

Reliability and scaling

  • Capacity is the main risk: reserve GPUs, diversify across zones and regions, and use queueing and priority for training versus inference.
  • Autoscale inference on queue depth and tokens per second; use continuous batching and quantisation to raise throughput.
  • Checkpoint training frequently so spot or node failures lose minutes, not days; use elastic and resumable jobs.
  • Canary and shadow deployments for new models with automatic rollback on evaluation regressions.

Cost levers

  • Utilisation is everything: share GPUs across workloads, batch requests, and right-size model size and precision.
  • Reserved capacity for steady inference, spot with checkpointing for training, scale to zero for rarely used endpoints.
  • Distil large models to smaller ones for high-volume tasks; track cost per million tokens by model and team.

Trade-offs and decisions

  • Hosted API versus self-hosted: hosted is fastest to ship and strongest on frontier quality; self-hosting gives control, data locality and cost advantages at scale.
  • Fine-tuning versus retrieval: retrieval is cheaper and updatable; fine-tune for style, format or domain behaviour.
  • Custom accelerators lower cost but require framework support and engineering; keep a portable path to GPUs.
  • Multi-provider models reduce concentration risk but increase evaluation and gateway complexity.
